# Attention science behind in-game alerts and HUD messages (fast action games)

Scope note: almost all of the eye tracking evidence found is from first-person shooters. No eye tracking study of a top-down or 3/4 view action game with real numbers was found (see Gaps under question 1). Where a finding is carried over to a top-down game, that is marked as an inference. Every figure below was read from the source named next to it unless a caveat says otherwise.

## 1. Where players look during action, and which screen regions are effectively blind in combat

### Takeaway
In shooters the aim point is the gaze anchor: in the one study with hard numbers, about 88% of fixations fell inside the central quarter of the screen, and the health, message box and score regions got about 2% of fixations. Better players stay even more centred, so HUD corners and edges are effectively unseen while fighting unless something pulls the eye there.

### Cited Findings
- Kenny, Koesling, Delaney, McLoone and Ward (2005, ECMS), a custom FPS on the Torque engine with an eye tracker: "the crosshair in a FPS game effectively creates a natural fixation point". "Near centre" was defined as the inner 400x300 rectangle of an 800x600 screen (the middle half of each axis, one quarter of the screen area). Nearly 88% of all fixations fell in that region, which was 86% of fixation time and 82% of game time. [Kenny et al. 2005, full text](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/282/1/Paper04_ECMS_2005.pdf)
- Same study: "the regions of the screen representing the user's health, message box and score received very little eye fixations, only 2% of all fixations", and one user alone accounted for 53% of those. [Kenny et al. 2005](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/282/1/Paper04_ECMS_2005.pdf)
- Same study, skill split: the three best players averaged 91% near-centre fixations with average fixation durations just over 700 ms; the worst players averaged 84% near-centre with fixations just under 470 ms. Most fixations were shorter than 300 ms. Twelve data sets were analysed. A later review describes the setup as six participants with an SR Research EyeLink 2 at 500 Hz. [Kenny et al. 2005](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/282/1/Paper04_ECMS_2005.pdf); [repository record](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/282/)
- Khromov et al. (arXiv 2018, "Esports Athletes and Players: a Comparative Study", CS:GO, 28 participants, about 30 min deathmatch with the radar disabled): "the players mainly look at the center of the screen where the aiming crosshair is located", and "the higher skill the player has, the more the gaze is concentrated on the screen center". Skilled players move the crosshair to an enemy quickly and then look back at the crosshair. [Khromov et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.03200)
- A 2020 student thesis built an FPS with a gaze-aware HUD (HUD elements hidden, notifications shown near the gaze point). The gaze HUD was rated about on par with a normal HUD; players preferred the normal, more visible ammo counter and health bar. Small student study, read from the search summary only. [Eye tracking complemented HUD for video games, 2020](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1509715/FULLTEXT02.pdf)
- Caroux, Le Bigot and Vibert (2011, Human Factors 53(2), 103 to 117): players anticipating obstacles that moved upward did better when the score was placed just outside the "anticipation zone", in the direction of anticipation, than when it overlapped that zone. Their design rule: contextual information should sit in the direction of anticipation but not inside the anticipation zone (an extension of the proximity-compatibility principle). [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51247061_Maximizing_Players'_Anticipation_by_Applying_the_Proximity-Compatibility_Principle_to_the_Design_of_Video_Games); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Maximizing-Players%E2%80%99-Anticipation-by-Applying-the-to-Caroux-Bigot/4eeb1565526a8cce82e16715432516b2d50dfd30)
- Caliber (1C Game Studios / Statehood, studio dev blog, 8 Aug 2023): the operator's HP, stamina, ability, weapon and ammo were moved near the centre "because elements closer to the center draw more attention"; the bottom panel mirrors the hands (movement info on the left, weapon info on the right); the kill feed sits next to enemy team info. Their UX tests also found a minimap made players communicate less and play solo. [Caliber, About our approach to HUD design, 2023](https://playcaliber.com/en/news/638/about-our-approach-to-hud-design/)
- Peacocke, Teather et al. (2015, IEEE GEM; 2018, Entertainment Computing): diegetic or spatial displays can sit centrally and cut how often players must glance at the screen edges. In 2015 an in-world ammo number beat HUD icons: about 35% fewer shots fired after running dry, 26% faster reloads, and 70% of participants preferred it. The 2018 follow-up (four experiments: ammo, health, weapon selection, navigation) found no single display type wins everywhere: diegetic or spatial best for ammo, the HUD better for health. (Figures from the project pages and search summaries; no eye tracking was reported.) [Peacocke et al. 2015](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/ieee_gem2015.html); [G-ScalE lab page](https://gscale.cas.mcmaster.ca/research/effectiveHUDS/); [Peacocke et al. 2018 PDF](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/ec2018.pdf); [2018 lab page](https://gscale.cas.mcmaster.ca/research/empiricalHUDs/)
- Yan and Seif El-Nasr (2006, ETRA poster, "Visual attention in 3D video games") report different visual attention patterns in an action-adventure game versus an FPS. The full text and numbers were not retrieved. [SFU Summit record](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/32); [ETRA 2006 listing](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/db/conf/etra/etra2006.html)
- Cao and Sweetser (July 2026, accepted to CHI PLAY 2026, 32 participants) studied a turn-based deck-building game, not a real-time one. Winners spread attention more evenly across functional areas and out to peripheral auxiliary resources. This suggests the centre lock is a product of real-time aiming pressure, not of games in general. [Cao and Sweetser 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17151)

### Inferences
- In a top-down 3/4 view shooter, the counterparts of the FPS crosshair are the player character and the mouse reticle (or stick aim line). The FPS data suggest that during fights gaze sits on the band between the character and the reticle, and screen corners get roughly 2% of glances.
- Corner and edge text (kill feeds, message logs, score tickers) is effectively invisible during combat. Anything that must be seen in combat has to go near the character or reticle, or be carried by sound or motion.
- Per Caroux 2011, a callout should sit next to the action, not on top of it: beside or just above the character, not over the space the player is aiming into.
- Skilled players fixate the centre more, so good players see even less of the periphery. A party demo crowd of mixed skill will vary, but the experts will miss corner text most.

### Gaps
- No eye tracking study of a top-down, twin-stick or 3/4 view action game with gaze percentages was found. The Yan and El-Nasr 2006 action-adventure numbers were not retrieved.
- No published gaze data from Ubisoft, Epic, Riot or Bungie user research was found.
- Kenny 2005 is small (six players, a simple custom FPS); Khromov 2018 gives heatmaps but no AOI percentages for HUD regions.

## 2. Peripheral vision: what can and cannot be read outside the gaze point, and what that means for text vs icons vs flashes

### Takeaway
The periphery is good at noticing flicker, motion and onsets, and bad at identifying letters, mainly because of crowding: nearby shapes merge once they are closer than about half the distance from the gaze point. Text in the periphery is a "something changed" signal at best; it is read only after the eyes move to it.

### Cited Findings
- Strasburger, Rentschler and Juttner (2011, Journal of Vision 11(5):13, review): peripheral vision is limited for pattern categorization "by a distinctly lower representational complexity and processing speed", and "the limitations of cognitive processing in peripheral vision appear to be as significant as those imposed on low-level functions and by way of crowding". [Strasburger et al. 2011](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/23648/1/jov_11_5_13.pdf)
- Same review, crowding: crowding is the loss of letter recognition when other contours are near. It occurs when flankers are closer than a critical distance given by Bouma's law (critical spacing about 0.5 times the target's eccentricity; the review's own data give Bouma factors of 0.625 to 0.7). There was "no reliable crowding effect in the fovea but a strong effect" in the periphery. A box drawn around a letter target ("supercrowding") cut accuracy by almost 50% even beyond Bouma's limit. [Strasburger et al. 2011](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/23648/1/jov_11_5_13.pdf)
- Same review, flicker and motion: critical flicker frequency increases with eccentricity out to about 60 degrees, and "the notion of a periphery that is more sensitive to flicker and motion also concurs with subjective experience" (a 50 Hz TV looks steady when viewed directly and flickers when viewed peripherally). The smallest detectable motion is about vernier acuity in the fovea, so motion can be seen between points too close to resolve. [Strasburger et al. 2011](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/23648/1/jov_11_5_13.pdf)
- Same review: attention and fixation can be decoupled (Helmholtz), and spatial attention modulates even low-level peripheral performance. [Strasburger et al. 2011](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/23648/1/jov_11_5_13.pdf)
- Pelli, Palomares and Majaj (2004, Journal of Vision 4(12):12): crowding harms identification, not detection, so a peripheral object can be seen to be there while its identity (for example, which word it is) cannot be read. [Pelli et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1167/4.12.12) (the claim is summarised in the review literature returned by search; the paper itself was not opened)
- Green and Bavelier (2003, Nature 423, 534 to 537): action video game play enhanced the capacity and spatial spread of visual attention, including the useful field of view (the area from which information can be pulled at a glance without moving the eyes), after as little as about 10 hours of training. A later study of 65 males (Murphy and Spencer) did not replicate the advantage. [Green and Bavelier 2003 PDF](https://psych.unl.edu/mdodd/Psy498/GreenBavelier.pdf); [Murphy and Spencer, JASNH](https://www.jasnh.com/pdf/Vol6-No1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Text placed away from the gaze anchor should be treated as unreadable until the player looks at it. To be useful, peripheral text needs a non-text "look here" cue (a brief motion, scale pulse or flash) and must stay up long enough for the eye to travel there and read it.
- Icons with one strong shape and colour survive the periphery far better than words, because detection survives crowding where identification does not. Rows of small text packed together (stacked logs, word strings next to icons) are the worst case for crowding.
- Panels and boxes drawn tightly around peripheral text may make it harder to read from the side (supercrowding); give peripheral text clear space rather than a tight frame.
- Constant flicker or animation in the periphery is itself a strong pull on the eyes, so any always-moving HUD element will steal glances from the fight. Reserve peripheral motion for things that deserve a glance.

### Gaps
- Colour sensitivity in the periphery was explicitly left out of the Strasburger review, and no game-specific study of peripheral colour coding was found.
- No study measured reading of short HUD words at given eccentricities during play.

## 3. Inattentional blindness and change blindness: evidence that players miss HUD text under load, and what cuts through

### Takeaway
Under high perceptual or cognitive load people miss fully visible but unexpected things, and they also miss alarm sounds (in one study about 29% of alarms were missed under low load and about 46% under high load). Direct game HUD evidence is thin; the best game evidence is observational (a brief text message was noticed by 1 participant). What reliably captures attention is the appearance of a new object (onset) and motion. Static, highlighted or merely coloured elements are ignored.

### Cited Findings
- Lavie's load theory, as tested in a 2020 study: under low perceptual load irrelevant stimuli tend to reach awareness, while under high load they tend to go undetected. That study found load affected the main task but did not interact with unconscious processing as predicted, so load does not gate everything simply. [Loads of unconscious processing, 2020 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7343742/); [Springer version](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-020-01982-8)
- Driving simulator studies (2016): high perceptual load increased inattentional blindness and slowed reactions to hazards, and also caused inattentional deafness. [Perceptual load induces inattentional blindness in drivers](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303353640_Perceptual_Load_Induces_Inattentional_Blindness_in_Drivers); [High perceptual load causes inattentional blindness and deafness in drivers](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283664063_High_perceptual_load_causes_inattentional_blindness_and_deafness_in_drivers)
- Inattentional deafness (2016, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, aviation-style task): 28.76% of alarms went unreported under low cognitive load and up to 46.21% under high cognitive load; perceptual load had no effect on the deafness rate. [The Role of Cognitive and Perceptual Loads in Inattentional Deafness (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4933714/)
- On-road AR HUD study (2025): the planar position of collision-warning graphics did not significantly change how often warnings were missed (inattentional blindness), and vertical placement gave the lowest workload. [Inattentional Blindness with Augmented Reality HUDs: An On-road Study, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.00879)
- Fagerholt and Lorentzon (2009, Chalmers master's thesis with EA DICE), user test on Resident Evil 5: breakable objects were flagged by "a brief barely noticeable textual message" and only one participant discovered the affordance the first time; an unflagged shelf-pushing action in an action scene was found by none; a "Jump through window" button prompt that only appeared when standing close was missed because players rarely got close enough. [Beyond the HUD, full text](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/111921.pdf); [Chalmers record](https://odr.chalmers.se/handle/20.500.12380/111921)
- Same thesis, focus group: overlay text instructions such as "Press X to pick up weapon" were said to break the experience in narrative games but were accepted in competition-focused games. [Beyond the HUD](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/111921.pdf)
- Caliber (2023 dev blog), from their UX testing: "players will ignore certain UI elements unless they're animated and highlighted"; animated or flashing elements attract more attention than static ones regardless of size; "too many visuals are effectively the same as none"; they alert on absence (something has run out) rather than presence, like a car's check engine light. [Caliber 2023](https://playcaliber.com/en/news/638/about-our-approach-to-hud-design/)
- Attentional capture by onset: Yantis and Jonides (1984, 1996) showed that a new letter appearing in an empty location captures attention in visual search. Yantis and Hillstrom (1994) found that a new object captures attention even when it is equiluminant with its background, so "newness", not just a luminance change, drives capture. In eye movement work, morphing an existing item was fixated first less often than a true onset. [Yantis and Jonides 1996 record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/107435); [Yantis and Hillstrom 1994 record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/5550575); [Sunny and von Muhlenen 2013, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00958/full); [Schreij et al. 2008](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/2326827/Schreij%20Perception%20and%20Psychophysics_%2070(2)%202008%20u.pdf)
- Change blindness and gamers: a 2009 Military Psychology study found habitual action game players were no better than non-players at change detection; a 2022 eye tracking study (36 participants, 9 game players) found no significant difference in change detection or eye movement measures. A small 2014 thesis (30 participants) did report a gamer advantage. [UCF record, 2009](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib2000/1494); [Influence of Video Game Playing on Change Detection: An Eye Tracking Study, 2022](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/60798696-73c8-4d53-a23b-ffea442a86d4); [Dublin Business School thesis, 2014](https://esource.dbs.ie/items/a34b7290-3744-45ab-bef8-6cbe20c9b6c8)
- Change blindness in a virtual arcade (2020 poster): only about 15% of changes made outside the participant's gaze were detected. Conference poster, weak source. [Change Blindness in a Virtual Arcade, 2020](https://encompass.eku.edu/swps/2020/faculty/12)

### Inferences
- A HUD message that just changes value in place (a counter ticking, a line of text swapping to another line) is a change blindness case and will mostly be missed in combat. A message that appears as a new object near the gaze anchor is an onset and has the best chance of being seen.
- Sound is not a free pass: under high cognitive load roughly half of alarms can be missed. Sound plus an onset near the character is stronger than either alone, and the most important alerts should get both.
- Keep motion for the few alerts that matter. If everything animates, motion stops working as a cue ("too many visuals are effectively the same as none").
- Alerting on absence or change of state (out of ammo, out of meds, extract open) rather than showing steady status keeps the number of things that move low.
- Gamers are not reliably better at noticing changes, so experienced players do not excuse a quiet or peripheral alert.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment measuring the miss rate of HUD text or toast notifications during real-time game combat was found.
- Celia Hodent's book and GDC talks were confirmed to cover attention and perception, but no quotable passage on inattentional blindness or players not reading text in combat could be retrieved (see question 5).
- No study of looming or scale-in motion as an alert cue in games was found.

## 4. Working memory and load: how many messages or HUD elements players can track, reading speed, minimum display times

### Takeaway
The best supported capacity limit is about four chunks held in mind at once (Cowan 2001), and players in a fight are already spending most of that on the fight. Subtitle standards give the only well established reading numbers: about 12 to 15 characters per second, at least 1 second per line, and up to about 6 or 7 seconds on screen.

### Cited Findings
- Cowan (2001, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1), 87 to 114): Miller's "seven" was a rough estimate; a wide range of data points to a central capacity limit "averaging about four chunks". Commentary in the same issue warned against taking capacity limits too literally. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/); [full text PDF](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf); [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/magical-number-4-in-shortterm-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496)
- Subtitle reading speed: a common rule of thumb is about 12 to 14 characters per second (about 180 words per minute). [Limecraft subtitle guide](https://support.limecraft.com/support/solutions/articles/48001249679) The EASIT project's subtitling instructions set a maximum of 15 characters per second (12 for children's content), a 1 second minimum and a 6 second maximum on screen. [EASIT subtitling instructions, 2021](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/infpro/2021/248286/EASIT_IO5_report._Annex_12._subtitling_instructions.pdf) An OTP/TED guide puts the ceiling for Latin-alphabet languages at 21 characters per second. [OTP Learning Series 08](https://amara.org/ca/videos/MVgIgNCU9zZQ/en/1149221/)
- Game subtitles (Canada Media Fund article citing the BBC): at most 38 characters per line, at most two lines (three only in exceptional cases), each line on screen for at least one second. Broadcast durations range from 2 to 5 seconds (BBC style) to 1 to 7 seconds (Netflix). [CMF, Subtitles in Video Games: The Best Practices](https://cmf-fmc.ca/now-next/articles/subtitles-in-video-games-the-best-practices/)
- A University of Barcelona subtitling guide gives a 1.5 second minimum, a 6 second maximum and 40 characters per line. [UB subtitling and captioning guide](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/128428/1/subtitling%20and%20captioning.pdf)
- Kenny et al. (2005): most FPS fixations lasted under 300 ms, and lower scoring players fixated more often for shorter times, which eye movement research ties to higher mental load. [Kenny et al. 2005](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/282/1/Paper04_ECMS_2005.pdf)
- Caliber (2023): extra HUD information led players to stop interacting with teammates, and less important elements are made smaller, dimmer and less dynamic. [Caliber 2023](https://playcaliber.com/en/news/638/about-our-approach-to-hud-design/)

### Inferences
- Treat three short callouts on screen at once as the practical ceiling in combat. With about four slots in working memory and the fight taking at least one, a fourth simultaneous message is likely to push another out, and a stack of five or more works like a log, not an alert.
- Display time for a callout can be set from subtitle reading rates plus the eye's travel time: about 1 second floor plus about 1 second per 12 to 15 characters. A 20 character callout comes to roughly 1.5 to 2.5 seconds for a player who looks straight at it. A callout away from the gaze anchor needs extra time for the glance (fixations in combat are mostly under 300 ms, so the glance itself is short; the delay is in deciding to look).
- Very short text (2 or 3 words) is the only text that fits the "read in a glance" budget during combat. Anything longer belongs to calm moments (hub, pause, after extraction).
- Six to seven seconds is the upper end of how long subtitle standards keep text up; past that a message has become standing HUD, not an alert.

### Gaps
- No game-specific study found that tested how many simultaneous HUD notifications players can track, or the miss rate as the stack grows.
- A search summary mentioned flight simulator work where both extremes of display clutter raised workload, but no primary source was opened, so it is not cited. A VR game clutter study exists but was not read: [Effects of environmental clutter and motion on user performance in VR games](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/effects-of-environmental-clutter-and-motion-on-user-performance-i/).
- No study of reading speed for short in-game callouts during play was found; the numbers above come from film and TV subtitling.
- No published fade-in or fade-out durations backed by research were found.

## 5. Practitioner and academic HUD sources: Hodent, studio user research talks, diegetic vs non-diegetic, HUD minimalism, notification overload

### Takeaway
Academic work supports a "HUD fades as skill grows" view: removing the HUD raised immersion for experts but not novices, and no display type wins for every kind of information. The best documented practitioner evidence found is the Caliber (2023) write-up. Hodent's work is confirmed to cover perception, memory and attention, but its specific guidance could not be quoted from primary text in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- Iacovides, Cox, Kennedy, Cairns and Jennett (2015, CHI PLAY, pp. 13 to 22), "Removing the HUD", Battlefield 3 with the HUD switched off by a console command. Study 1: 9 male participants. Study 2: 24 participants (10 experts, 14 novices; experts played FPS at least an hour a week), between subjects; 7 more were excluded because they struggled with the controls. Interactions of expertise and interface: Cognitive Involvement F(1,20) = 7.80, p < 0.05, partial eta squared 0.280; Control F(1,20) = 10.05, p < 0.01, partial eta squared 0.334. Overall immersion (IEQ): experts 133.83 without the HUD vs 119.00 with it; novices 124.50 vs 126.25. The authors: "The HUD may initially provide important information to players, but can later act as a barrier to deeper levels of involvement when the player has gained sufficient expertise". Caveat: one level of one game. [Iacovides et al. 2015, full text](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1470398/1/chip0179-iacovidesA.pdf); [Open University record](https://oro.open.ac.uk/46759); [CHI PLAY Lasting Impact award (York)](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/prizes/chi-play-lasting-impact-award/)
- Fagerholt and Lorentzon (2009, Chalmers, with EA DICE): classify UI elements as diegetic, non-diegetic, spatial or meta, using two questions (is it in the story, is it in the game space); first guideline "Know Your Design Space"; competition-focused games should make conveyed information unambiguous. In their survey of games, 14% of UI elements found were auditory and 9% haptic, the rest visual. They also report Breda's view that peripheral HUD information can pull the eye from the focal point, though players accept the HUD. [Beyond the HUD full text](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/111921.pdf); [Game Developer, UI design in video games](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/user-interface-design-in-video-games)
- Peacocke et al. (2015, 2018): no single display type is best; diegetic or spatial best for ammo, HUD best for health (details under question 1). [2018 PDF](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/ec2018.pdf)
- Caroux and Isbister (2016, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 87): studied how HUD physical and semantic characteristics affect user experience in action games; a later review cites its report of novice fixation durations on the HUD. The findings were behind a paywall and not retrieved. [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581915001779); [Loic Caroux publications](https://sites.google.com/site/loiccaroux/publications)
- Caroux et al. (2013, Ergonomics): background motion and visual complexity affect performance in game-like displays (title and focus only). [Influence of visual background complexity and task difficulty on action video game players' performance](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/408DdWv4)
- Celia Hodent, "The Gamer's Brain: How Neuroscience and UX Can Impact Video Game Design" (CRC Press, 2017): chapters on perception, memory and attention, then a UX framework built on usability and "engage-ability". She worked on UX at Epic Games, Ubisoft and LucasArts. [Routledge](https://routledge.com/9781498775502)
- Hodent, "The Gamer's Brain, Part 2: UX of Onboarding and Player Engagement" (GDC 2016, Epic Games), covers onboarding pitfalls and guidelines drawn from how the brain learns, with Fortnite examples; free on the GDC YouTube channel. [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1022951/The-Gamer-s-Brain-Part); [Game Developer write-up](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-inside-the-ux-of-onboarding-and-player-engagement)
- Caliber (2023): four zones (top: state of battle; bottom: own operator; left: allies; right: enemies), the most important stats near the centre, shapes with fixed meanings (circles for positive interactive objects, diamonds for goals and negative objects), colour-blind schemes, and every element checked over light, dark and mixed backgrounds. [Caliber 2023](https://playcaliber.com/en/news/638/about-our-approach-to-hud-design/)

### Inferences
- A minimal raid HUD that grows quieter as players learn the game is supported by the expert result in Iacovides 2015. Novices (party guests who have never played) still need some non-diegetic help, so the safest pattern is "show early, fade later", not "never show".
- Information that is read during action and changes often (ammo, reload) works best on or near the player (diegetic or spatial); slow status that is checked deliberately (health total, money) can stay in fixed HUD.
- Notification overload is mostly a practitioner finding (Caliber), not an academic one: the more things compete, the less each one is noticed.

### Gaps
- No primary text from Hodent's book or talks could be quoted on inattentional blindness, multitasking or text in combat; a follow-up should pull the GDC 2015 or 2016 talk transcript or the attention chapter.
- No GDC Vault talk on HUD or notifications from Bungie (Destiny), Blizzard (Overwatch), Respawn (Apex), Epic (Fortnite) or Guerrilla (Horizon) with retrievable numbers was found in this pass. A search for kill feed and notification priority talks returned only general blog posts.
- No academic study specifically on notification overload or toast stacking in games was found.

## 6. Specific numbers: durations, fades, lines, text size relative to screen height, contrast

### Takeaway
Hard numbers exist for text size and contrast (Xbox Accessibility Guidelines) and for subtitle timing and line limits (broadcast standards). No research-backed numbers were found for alert fade-in or fade-out times or for alert duration in action games.

### Cited Findings
- Xbox Accessibility Guideline 101 (text display, XAG v3.2, June 2023): minimum default body text height (descender to ascender) is 26 px at 1080p and 52 px at 4K on console (10 foot viewing), and 18 px at 1080p and 36 px at 4K on PC. Players should be able to scale text up to 200% (icons and glyphs scale with it); text smaller than default only if the player opts in. Lines at most 80 characters (40 for CJK); line spacing at least 1.5x; letter spacing at least 0.12x; word spacing at least 0.16x. Coloured outline pixels only count toward contrast if they exceed 2:1 against the background. Display duration is not covered. ### Inferences
- As a share of 1080p screen height: 18 px (Xbox PC minimum) is about 1.7%; 26 px (Xbox console minimum) is about 2.4%; 36 px (Xbox PC "large", where 3:1 contrast is allowed) is about 3.3%; 46 px (Game Accessibility Guidelines subtitles) is about 4.3%; 86 px (BBC line height) is about 8%. For a game played on a TV at a party, the console figure (26 px or more at 1080p, about 2.4% of height) is the safer floor than the PC one.
- Over a busy game scene, 4.5:1 contrast is only reliable with a plate or a solid outline, because the darkest or lightest patch of ground sets the ratio.
- Duration and fade numbers have to come from the reading-rate inference in question 4 and from playtests; no research value was found to copy.

### Gaps
- No research-based figure for alert fade-in or fade-out time, or for how fast an alert should animate in, was found.
- XAG 104 (subtitles) was not retrieved, so any Xbox rule on subtitle duration or line count is unconfirmed.
- No study found that ties alert size (as a fraction of screen height) to detection rate during gameplay.
